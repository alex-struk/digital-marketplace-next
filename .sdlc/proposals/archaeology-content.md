# Is this what the content domain does, and which of it is the contract?

**Recommendation.** **Journal — archaeology of the `content` domain**

The content domain is how the old application keeps its own prose out of its own code.

**Journal — archaeology of the `content` domain**

The content domain is how the old application keeps its own prose out of its own code. Every piece of standing text the service shows — its terms and conditions, its privacy, accessibility, copyright and disclaimer notices, the scope and evaluation notes embedded inside an opportunity, the instructions an evaluation panel reads, even the markdown help offered to authors — is a page with a title, a body of formatted text and a short address, readable by anyone at that address and editable only by an administrator through a management area reached from the navigation menu. Pages come in two kinds: ordinary ones an administrator creates and removes at will, and pages the service itself depends on, which are created by database migration, may be re-worded but never renamed or removed, and arrive on a fresh installation carrying placeholder text — twenty-two of them, each titled by its own address and all saying "Initial version" until somebody writes them.

I recovered twenty-eight criteria into `spec/domains/content.md`: eight `confirmed` where the release notes, the schema description or the manual smoke-test script corroborate the code, nineteen `inferred` where only the code shows the behaviour, and one `open`. Four pages went into `spec/contract/surface.yaml` under `domain: content` — the list, the create form, the per-page management screen and the public page. I added nothing to `personas.yaml`: the only two roles this domain distinguishes are an administrator, who may do everything, and any visitor at all including one who is not signed in, who may read; both are already listed, and duplicating them under a second domain would have collided rather than added.

What conflicted. The two mismatches worth a ruling are both inside the code rather than between code and prose. Seven pages the service creates for itself — the opportunity and proposal guides of all three programs and one opportunity scope page — are linked from nowhere in the application; the explanatory material they were meant to hold now lives in hard-coded "learn more" screens, but the pages remain, marked as needed by the service so an administrator cannot remove them, and the list of pages gives no hint they are unused. In the opposite direction, five places link to a "service level agreement" page that no migration ever creates. The published interface description is a third kind of disagreement: it documents a dozen other parts of the service and says nothing about pages at all, so everything below the screen level rests on the code alone. Two smaller inconsistencies are recorded as defects: a refusal to create or change a page for lack of permission comes back shaped like a complaint about the submission, while a refused request for the list of pages comes back as a permission refusal; and a page's body is rendered on its own address without filtering what its markup may contain, while the same body embedded in another screen is not — so an author previewing a page cannot tell which rendering another screen will use.

What I could not determine. Whether the service-level-agreement link is actually broken in a running installation is unresolvable from the source: an administrator can create an ordinary page at that address by hand, and a long-lived deployment may well have one. That is why I graded it `open` rather than asserting a break — but either way the page would be an ordinary one, removable and absent from a fresh installation, unlike every other page the service links to itself. I also could not consult the old application's commit history: the environment refused the read, so the questions history would have settled — when the guide pages fell out of use, whether the service-level-agreement page was ever seeded and then dropped — are unanswered rather than answered negatively. And two behaviours I recorded as `inferred` are reasoned from the code's structure rather than observed: that a rename leaves nothing at the old address, and that two administrators editing the same page at once produce a silent last-writer-wins overwrite, since the submission carries no record of which version it was based on. Both are worth exercising against the running old application before anyone treats them as settled.

## Ruling

**Verdict:** approve
**By:** agent:product-owner

The question was whether this is what the content domain does and which of it is the contract, and the recovered account holds up under checking: the seeded-page count is exactly 22 across five migrations and the note's breakdown is correct slug for slug; the corroborations behind every 'confirmed' grade say at the cited lines what they are claimed to say; and the citations are line-exact throughout. I approve it. Four criteria I am raising to confirmed because a second reading of the code leaves no other interpretation, not because the proposal asked me to: D-content-1 is tipped by the database layer rather than the resource — generateContentQuery (db/content.ts:67-97) applies no session predicate to rows at all and varies only whether the authorship columns are selected, so public readability is now observed in two independent places rather than inferred from a missing permission check; D-content-3, D-content-4 and D-content-5 are single-path logic inside one handler (content.ts:76-106) that I read end to end, where the identifier-then-address fallback, the 400 on a malformed address and the 404 on a missing page are the only three exits. Three behaviours should not be carried into the rebuild. D-content-22 is the most serious thing in this proposal: view.tsx:118 passes escapeHtml={false}, which markdown.tsx:144 turns into rehypeRaw, so a page body is rendered as unfiltered markup on its own address and as literal text everywhere it is embedded — page authorship is today an administrator-only power, which is why this is a defect rather than an incident, but a rebuild that copies it inherits stored-markup execution plus a preview that lies about which rendering another screen will use. D-content-18 is the same shape at lower stakes: content.ts:55 answers a refused list with a permission refusal while :130-137 answers a refused create as though the submission were faulty, and the rebuild should be consistent. D-content-27 I am marking obsolete rather than spiking, because the evidence is now complete — every fixed page the application actually uses is named by a constant in front-end/typescript/config.ts:37-59, and the six program guides and team-with-us-opportunity-scope have none and no reference anywhere outside their migrations — so seeding seven unremovable pages nothing links to is a behaviour to drop deliberately, not a question; existing rows in a long-lived deployment are untouched by this, since J5 keeps the schema. D-content-28 I am raising to confirmed and ruling a defect, which is a departure from the 'open' grade the proposal gave it, and the reason is that the grade was attached to the wrong proposition: the statement as written is a fact about the source, and I checked it independently — five contentView links to 'service-level-agreement' and zero occurrences in the complete 22-slug seed set. What is genuinely unresolvable from the source is only whether a particular running installation happens to have an administrator-made page there, and that is a data-migration question rather than a spec fact, so it should not hold the criterion at 'open'. The rebuild must not ship five links to an address it does not guarantee; since the content is wanted beside cost-recovery information for public sector employees, the fix is to seed it like every other page the service links to itself. D-content-24 I am spiking rather than confirming: the proposal is honest that a silent last-writer-wins overwrite is reasoned from the submission carrying no base version rather than observed, and the consequence is silently reverted wording that D-content-13 leaves invisible to the person overwritten, so it needs exercising against the running old application per J7 before anyone builds to it. D-content-14 I am leaving exactly as recovered at 'inferred' — the slug is the lookup key and no alias table exists, so it is structurally near-certain, and dropping it to 'open' would be pessimism rather than honesty. Nothing here contradicts J2: this is specification work against a read-only reference. What would change this ruling is evidence that any confirmed grade rests on a corroboration that does not hold at its cited line, or a demonstration that the seeded-slug set is larger than the five migrations I counted.
